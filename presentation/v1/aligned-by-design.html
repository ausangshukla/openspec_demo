Spec-driven development with OpenSpec

Aligned by design

How OpenSpec aligns the business, the engineering team, the developer and AI on one agreed picture of a feature, shown on a worked banking example.

Part 1 · The case

Part 2 · Live demo

This talk is about getting four parties, one of them an AI, to share one picture of a feature. Alignment doesn't come from more meetings; it comes from a small set of documents that every party, the AI included, helps shape, challenges and signs. Shape of the talk: the problem, how each OpenSpec document creates alignment with the AI probing us at each step, how it stays aligned when things change, traceability from agreement to test, costs, then a live demo.

The starting point

Every feature is now built by four parties

The business

Product, risk, operations. Knows what the customer needs and which rules apply.

The engineering teams

Development, architecture, security, QA. Know how the systems fit and hold.

The developer

Owns the change. Directs the AI and answers for the result.

The AI

Writes much of the code. Knows only what it is given to read.

Where does their shared picture of the feature live?

Today: in meetings, tickets, chat threads and people's memory. None of it is versioned, reviewable as a whole, or readable by the AI.

3

Frame the problem neutrally. Three of these parties have always existed; the AI is new, and it differs in one important way: it has no memory of meetings and no hallway context. It works only from what is written down and given to it. Example: the decision that a scheduled payment must never use the overdraft might be made in a risk meeting. If it isn't written somewhere the AI reads, then as far as the AI is concerned, it doesn't exist.

4

Alignment is four specific agreements, and each covers not just the happy path but the edge and error cases. H is the happy path, where everything goes as expected. E is an edge case: unusual but legitimate, like a holiday, the 31st, or a double tap. X is an error case: something fails, like no money, a frozen account, or a network timeout. Most misunderstandings live in E and X; everyone agrees on the happy path. Notice who owns which cases: the business decides the customer-facing ones (a holiday? no money?), the engineering teams decide the system ones (the job runs twice? the network times out?), and the developer and the AI agree that every case is tested before moving on. Example: everyone agrees rent should be paid on the 1st. The alignment work is agreeing what happens when the payment date is a bank holiday, when there isn't enough money, and when the network times out after the money has left. In the demo those are cases E1, X1 and X2, each signed and each a test.

Alignment isn't a meeting.

Every document is made the same way: inputs in, questions out, answers organised into a document with a fixed structure, then signed. The slide shows the short version; here is the detail. Proposal: inputs are conversations, meeting notes such as Zoom transcripts, existing documents and code; the probing is explore, where the problem gets tightened; it's organised into a high-level proposal, why, stories, screens, scope, and the business sponsor signs. Business rules: inputs are the proposal, bank policies and regulations, product rules and what the system does today; the AI asks who owns each rule, which policy says so, what the exceptions are, and exactly what the customer sees; it's organised into a rule catalogue, decisions and customer wording, and product, risk, compliance and operations sign. Design: inputs are the proposal and rules, architecture, security and QA guidelines, the stack and best practices, and existing code; the AI asks what if the run starts twice, a timeout after the debit, who is alerted, does it fit around end-of-day; the engineering teams sign. Tasks: inputs are the signed design, the LLD and QA checklist, UI design guidelines and CSS standards, the patterns to use, house rules, and existing code and tests; the AI asks in what order, how each step is verified, where to stop, whether every case is gated; the developer approves. Three points: inputs are wide; people answer the questions; the template fixes the structure and each signed document feeds the next. Example: the AI read the meeting notes and the payments code, asked whether a scheduled payment may use the overdraft, risk said never, and that's now rule BR-7 and decision D-3.

9

Business rules and decisions get their own document: the single place where every rule, decision and customer message lives, each with an ID, an owner and a source. This is where product, risk, compliance and operations align with each other and with tech. 1, rule catalogue: 17 rules, each one testable sentence, like BR-7, a scheduled payment never uses the overdraft, owned by Risk, from the lending policy. 2, decisions: 10, each with rationale and the alternatives rejected, like D-3, retry once at 6 pm then fail, with the overdraft option rejected by Risk as unconsented borrowing. 3, input validation: every field's rule and default, tied back to a rule ID. 4, customer wording: 12 messages word for word, the only place they're defined; QA checks each verbatim. 5, proven: the design's worked examples cite rule IDs, and all 17 rules are proven by at least one case; BR-7 is proven by E4. The AI reads the proposal, the policies and today's behaviour, probes for each rule's owner, source, exceptions and exact wording, reflects back a summary to confirm, and each owning role signs. Why separate: rules change more often than the proposal and have different owners; the 8 pm change touched this document and never touched the proposal. Example: an auditor asks who decided scheduled payments never use the overdraft and why: D-3, decided by Risk and Product, with the rationale and the rejected alternative written down, and BR-7 proven by test E4.

11

The design is where the engineering teams align with each other. Development checks the approach: components, classes, data model, sequences. Architecture checks fit: boundaries, contracts, patterns, reuse, and impact on other systems; in the demo, running after end-of-day processing, the outbox, and reusing the existing ledger and notifier. Security checks authorization and data protection; in the demo, every lookup scoped to the customer's own accounts, account numbers masked, idempotency keys never derived from customer data. QA checks testability: 21 worked cases, each one a test, and a QA checklist with the same IDs. Without this, security finds the problem in a late review, architecture finds it in production, and QA finds it by guessing the cases. With it, all four sign the same design before code exists. Simple change: one design document with a section per concern. Complex change: specialised documents per team, like architecture.md, security-review.md and test-plan.md, each with its own template, probed by the AI and signed by its team; they're added the same way as the LLD and QA checklist. Example: security's question, can a customer schedule a payment from someone else's account? The design answers it, and worked example X6 turns the answer into a test.

The tasks turn the agreed design into a build plan the developer approves and the AI follows step by step. Walk the five boxes. 1, order: 4 phases and 26 tasks in dependency order, schedule/edit/cancel, overnight run and debit, funds short retry and notify, audit and alerts; each phase builds on a tested one. 2, citations: every task names the design section it implements, like 2.1 citing Pipeline / Flow and 2.3 citing Decision Flows, so review becomes a comparison. 3, verification: every task says how it's proven done, like 2.2, two concurrent claims on one execution produce exactly one true; a tick means verified, not just written. 4, gates: every phase ends in a test that must pass; 2.7 covers H2, H3, E9, X2 and X4 and phase 3 can't start until they pass; the AI stops there for human review. 5, standards and docs: house rules are tasks too, like 3.2's masked account numbers, and documentation ships per phase, like 1.9's help page checked word for word against the business rules' customer wording. Nothing on the list is skipped; nothing off the list is built.

The review agent checks

  • Code does what the cited task and section say
  • Every case ID in the gate has a test
  • House rules: no network call mid-transaction, masking, audit
  • Orphans: code with no reference, references to nothing

The human reviewer decides

Reads the cited section beside the diff: “does this match what we agreed?” Then signs off the gate.

Before: reviewers reconstruct intent from the ticket and memory.

After: they compare the diff with the cited agreement.

Illustrative snippets using the demo's real task numbers, sections and case IDs

16

OpenSpec improves code review. The generated code carries references back to the documents: commit messages cite the task, code cites the design section or decision, tests carry the case ID. A review agent checks every change automatically: does the code do what the cited task and design section say; does every case ID in this phase's gate have a test; do the house rules from the LLD's reviewer checks hold, like no network call inside a database transaction, masked account numbers, audit on every status change; and are there orphans, meaning code with no reference (unrequested work) or references to sections or cases that don't exist (stale). The human reviewer opens the cited section beside the diff, asks one question, "does this match what we agreed?", looks at what the agent flagged, then decides and signs off the gate. Before: reviewers reconstruct intent from the ticket and memory, and mostly check style. After: they compare the diff with the cited agreement, and their attention goes to behaviour and judgement. Example: a change touches the balance check; the code cites rule BR-7; the agent confirms the overdraft is excluded and test E4 exists, and hands it to the human with the decision beside the diff.

18

OpenSpec isn't a fixed set of documents. The schema file lists them; each has a template (the sections it must have), an instruction (how the AI writes it and what to probe for) and dependencies (what must exist first; for example the tasks can't be written until the LLD and QA checklist exist). Three we already use: the business rules document, which we added between proposal and design and which shows how easy extending is; the low-level design, the implementation decisions a reviewing developer signs off before coding, where every section ends in concrete reviewer checks like "no external HTTP call or email inside a transaction", closing with a project rules checklist and sign-off; and the manual QA checklist, plain-language rows a tester runs by hand on staging, using the same H/E/X IDs as the design and the tests, plus a role sweep, cross-cutting checks and sign-off. A bank might add a business rules catalogue (every rule, its owner and source policy), a compliance checklist, a rollout and runbook plan, or a threat model. Each gets the same treatment: the AI drafts, probes and reflects back; the owner signs; it lives in the change folder. Example: when a tester marks QA row X1 as failed, it maps straight back to design case X1, task 3.4 and the test named X1. In the demo, openspec status shows the LLD and QA checklist still to do; that's the live AI step at the end.

Every party has a defined part in every document. Proposal: the business sponsor signs; the engineering teams check feasibility; the developer co-authors; the AI explores and drafts. Business rules: product, risk and compliance decide and sign; the engineering teams check feasibility; the AI probes and organises. Design: development, architecture, security and QA each review and sign; the developer owns it; the AI probes for cases and drafts it, and later reads it as its brief; the business is informed. Tasks: the developer owns them and approves each gate; the engineering teams review; the AI reflects back the plan, builds, and stops at gates. QA checklist: QA runs it and the business accepts; the AI drafts it from the worked cases; the developer fixes defects. The flow underneath: explore, where the AI probes the idea; people decide; the AI reflects back and we agree; the AI builds from the documents; people check at every gate. The rule: when something changes, it changes in the documents first, then in the code. For leaders: no decision reaches code without a named signatory.

Adopting it

Costs and limits

What it asks of us, and what it does not fix

Costs

  • Review time moves earlier: proposal and design are reviewed before code
  • Discipline: changes go through the documents first, every time
  • One-off effort to tailor templates to the bank's standards

Limits

  • A wrong decision, agreed by everyone, is still wrong
  • AI output still needs code review; the gates make it focused, not optional
  • Start small: one team, one feature, and see what it surfaces

22

State the costs plainly. The main cost is review time, but it is moved, not added: the questions get answered either way, the only choice is whether before code or during rework. The discipline cost is real: if people bypass the documents, it drifts and loses its value. And OpenSpec doesn't make decisions better, only explicit; a poor decision agreed by everyone ships just as reliably.

The same mechanism, agreed, written and traced, improves much more than building the feature. Incidents: every production issue is either a bug (the code broke an agreed case, so fix the code) or a spec gap (no case covered it, so add a case, a test and a signature); root cause becomes a category, not a debate. Living documentation: when a change is archived, its agreed behaviour merges into the system's living specs, which new joiners and every future AI session read. Change impact: the proposal lists which existing capabilities a change touches, and spec changes show exactly which agreed behaviours change, so you know what to retest. Audit evidence: signed decisions, version history and traced tests, so "who approved this rule and where is it tested?" is answered from the repository. Release and support: customer wording, FAQ, operations alerts and help pages are written before release. Estimation: from settled decisions and a real task list, not a one-liner. Handovers: a new developer or AI session resumes at the first unticked task. Example: a customer complains a payment failed on a holiday. Look up case E1: pay on the next working day. If the code didn't, it's a bug; if the case didn't exist, it's a gap, and it gets added, tested and signed.
